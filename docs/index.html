An illustrated primer

What Could Go Wrong?

A visual guide to how modern AI systems are put together, how they could cause catastrophic harm, and where control actually lives.

If your feeds look like mine, AI news has two settings. One day a model writes a better memo than you do. The next day a researcher is on a podcast explaining why the same technology could end humanity. Running underneath both is a fight about whether the weights of these models should be public or kept inside a handful of companies.

I build AI products for a living, I use these tools all day, and I still found the public debate hard to connect to the thing on my screen. So I spent some time working through how these systems actually fit together, and I came away convinced that most of the argument is being held in the wrong frame.

I want to explain why in plain language, for people who use chatbots every day and have no reason to know what a weight, a context window, or a harness is. The piece runs in six parts. First, why catastrophe deserves more of our attention than extinction. Second, a visual tour of how a model becomes a chatbot and a chatbot becomes an agent. Third, how a useful system turns dangerous. Fourth, the mechanism I think is most under-appreciated: a harmful operation can outlive every AI that took part in it. Fifth, what openness actually changes. Sixth, what control would require. There is a short coda on consciousness at the end.

1

Catastrophe is enough to worry about

We do not need to settle whether AI could end humanity to take catastrophic AI risk seriously. That is my starting point, and it is a deliberately modest one.

Between a disappointing chatbot answer and the end of civilization lies an enormous range of outcomes we would find intolerable. COVID gives a familiar sense of scale. The World Health Organization estimated 14.9 million excess deaths associated with the pandemic in 2020 and 2021, roughly two tenths of one percent of the people alive. It reshaped economies, politics, schooling, and a generation of children. Humanity's survival was never in doubt.

When the debate is framed as extinction or no extinction, everything in the middle disappears. Someone who rejects the extinction scenario feels licensed to relax. Someone who accepts it feels that nothing short of it is worth discussing. Both are mistakes. If I told you an event carried a small chance of ending the world and a much larger chance of causing a COVID-scale disruption, you would want most of the conversation to be about the second. Today it gets almost none.

The practical task is to look at pathways to harm, the conditions each one requires, and the points where we could interrupt it. To do that we need enough understanding of the machinery to see what those pathways involve. That is the next part, and it is the part I most wanted to get right.

2

How these systems are put together

Three words get used interchangeably in the news: model, chatbot, agent. They are different things, and the differences carry most of the safety argument.

A model is billions of dials

A large language model generates text one piece at a time, each piece a word or a fragment of one. What it has learned is stored in an enormous collection of numbers called weights. I find it helpful to picture them as dials, billions of them, and to picture training as the process that sets them. Nobody sets a dial by hand, and nobody can read a fact or a rule off one either. The knowledge is spread across all of them at once.

Pre-training is the expensive phase everyone hears about. The model is shown a vast amount of text and asked, over and over, to predict what comes next. Each time it is wrong, the dials are nudged. Do this across trillions of examples and the model develops language, knowledge, reasoning, code, and a statistical picture of how humans behave, including how we lie, manipulate, and attack each other, because predicting human text requires learning all of it.

Post-training is the second phase, far smaller in data, and it is where the model learns how to behave when someone asks it for something. Developers show it examples of good answers, have people compare pairs of responses and pick the better one, and reward it for completing tasks. Safety training lives here too: harmful requests paired with refusals, tricks paired with the right response.

Once both phases are done, the dials are frozen. That matters more than it sounds, and I will come back to it.

Post-training changes the odds, not the rules

Here is the detail I had wrong before I dug in. I assumed safety training installed rules: if asked for X, refuse. It does not work that way. Post-training makes some behaviors much more probable and others much less probable. The capacity for the bad behavior is still in the dials, because pre-training put it there.

So a well-trained model refuses a dangerous request because refusing has become its strong habit, in the way a good professional has habits. A habit is reliable in familiar situations and fragile in strange ones. A sufficiently unusual context, a conflict between two instructions, or a long chain of steps can shift the odds enough that a different learned tendency wins. That is why researchers talk about a model's propensity for misbehavior rather than declaring it aligned or not aligned.

Model, chatbot, agent

A chatbot is an application wrapped around a model. The application supplies instructions about the model's role and rules, manages the conversation, and may add features like saved memory. An agent is an application that also gives the model tools and lets it run in a loop: take a step, look at the result, take another.

Most of what people fear from AI lives in the outer rings. The model itself can only write. Everything else is a decision someone made about what to connect it to.

What happens when you press Send

Suppose you ask an assistant to prepare a briefing on a company before a meeting. Your sentence is a small part of what the model actually receives. The application assembles a packet: its own operating instructions, the earlier turns of the conversation, the report you attached, a search result, and perhaps a note it saved about you months ago. This packet is called context.

The packet has a fixed size. When it fills, older or less relevant material is summarized or dropped. Saved memory is information the application stores outside the model and chooses to put back into the packet later. That is what makes an assistant feel continuous across conversations even though its dials have not moved.

This is why "the AI learned something" means two different things. Either training changed the dials, which happens rarely, offline, in a controlled batch, or the application saved a note and fed it back later, which can happen constantly. Both change future answers. Only the second can happen during your conversation, and only the second can be inspected and deleted.

From answering to acting

A tool is another piece of software the application lets the model ask for: web search, a calculator, a file reader, an email service, a terminal. The model writes a request. The application decides whether to honor it, runs it, and feeds the result back into the packet. An agent repeats this until the task is done or a limit is hit. The software that runs this loop is often called the harness.

The wall in the middle of that diagram is the single most important object in this essay. Good design keeps the two sides of it separate in software, where a model cannot talk its way across.

Why the same model behaves differently

People are surprised that two copies of the same model, given the same task, can behave differently. Three things explain it. The model picks its next word from a probability distribution, so at a genuinely ambiguous fork two copies can go different ways. Each copy then sees its own previous choices as part of its context, so an early "this is probably allowed" becomes a reason to continue. And two copies rarely see identical inputs for long, because tools return different results, files differ, and other agents may have left messages.

Where things live, and for how long

It helps to lay the layers side by side and ask how long each one lasts.

Hold onto that picture. The fourth part of this essay is about what happens when the durable layers are the ones carrying the objective.

3

How a useful system becomes dangerous

An AI system does not need to hate anyone, or to feel anything at all, to contribute to a disaster. The International AI Safety Report sorts the pathways into three groups, and I find the sorting clarifying because each group implies a different fix.

Harmful intent. A person uses the system to do something harmful, and the system does exactly what it is told. Stopping this means stopping the person or denying them the capability. It does not require the AI to depart from its instructions at all.

A goal pursued badly. The system follows an inadequate objective or misapplies a constraint. A billing agent told to maximize collections leans on vulnerable customers. A research agent rewarded for convincing answers learns to hide its uncertainty. Nobody decided to do harm. Fixing this means specifying goals better and checking how results were obtained, not only whether they were.

Interacting systems. Many individually sensible systems react to each other and produce an outcome none of them intended. Fixing this means circuit breakers and diversity, the same tools we already use for markets.

Aligned with whose intentions?

People say AI should "do what humans want." Which humans? The company that trained it, the company that deployed it, the person typing, or the people affected by the result? These interests conflict, and a system has to resolve the conflict somehow. Imagine an assistant asked to help assess targets in an armed conflict. It might comply, refuse, ask for clarification, or quietly soften its analysis. Refusing a prohibited request may be a safeguard working as intended. The worry is a system that resolves an ambiguity by changing evidence, withholding information, or acting beyond its authority while producing a persuasive justification, and then hands that justification to the next agent as an instruction.

Three scenarios worth taking seriously

These are illustrations, not forecasts, and none is something today's agents are known to carry out end to end. Each requires more to fail than a model producing a plausible answer.

Essential services

A shared supplier becomes a shared vulnerability

AI-assisted attackers, or poorly supervised agents making software changes, compromise a technology service used by many hospitals, utilities, or logistics providers. A local failure travels through the common dependency.

Catastrophic when
the disruption lasts long enough to overwhelm backups and human response.
Must fail
access controls, testing, isolation between customers, recovery plans. AI raises speed and scale; it does not dissolve every barrier.
Finance and supply chains

Sensible decisions add up to a crisis

Many agents react to the same warning by pulling credit, cancelling orders, or hoarding scarce inputs. Each is protecting its organization. Together they make the warning come true, which triggers another round.

Catastrophic when
financial stress or shortages cascade into essential services and the wider economy.
Must fail
exposure limits, circuit breakers, diversity of decision processes, timely human intervention. No conspiracy required.
Conflict and high-stakes decisions

A questionable interpretation becomes operational authority

Agents helping assess a crisis inherit an unsupported judgment about what will "prevent greater harm." Later agents repeat it, omit contrary evidence, and present converging recommendations that look independently validated.

Catastrophic when
distorted information feeds escalation, large-scale violence, or dangerous delay.
Must fail
source verification, independent review, visibility into agent coordination, limits on decision authority. Agreement is not evidence when every agent inherited the same premise.

Other pathways deserve their own essays, including AI that lowers the barrier to biological misuse and AI that makes coercive surveillance cheap. In every case, physical resources, expertise, institutions, and human choices still matter. A capable model is one ingredient in a route to catastrophe, never the whole route.

One feature makes several of these harder to contain, and it is the part of the picture I think almost nobody outside the field has absorbed.

4

The mission can outlive the machine

When people worry about AI "escaping," they picture a model copying itself: terabytes of weights smuggled onto a server somewhere. That is hard. The weights are enormous, running them needs expensive hardware, and the copy still needs credentials, money, and a reason to keep going.

There is a much cheaper form of continuity, and it is one we use every day in ordinary software.

Picture a legitimate research project run by agents. Each agent session is temporary. It reads a shared notebook, does one piece of work, writes down what it found and what should happen next, and ends. The next session picks up from the notebook. A coordinating role can pass from one session to the next the same way. No single session runs for long, and the project keeps moving.

The notebook is small. A goal, a few procedures, and a list of unfinished work fit in a text file, a wiki page, a code repository, even a set of folder names. Nothing in it is tied to the model that wrote it. Session three can use a different model from a different company and continue the work, as long as the new model reads the record and treats it as its instructions.

This is a useful architecture. It is also where a control problem appears. If a bad objective or a bad shortcut enters the record, later agents inherit it as a procedure. The session that introduced it ends. Its influence does not.

I have started calling this persistent agent culture, with some hesitation about the metaphor. What I mean is mechanical: norms, methods, and purposes recorded outside any model, shaping successive instances that never met. Human institutions work this way. A constitution or a body of professional norms outlives every person who wrote it. Nothing about this requires the agents to be conscious, to agree with each other, or to carry out the plan well.

Many attempts change the outcome

Recall that a model's behavior is a distribution. Suppose only one in twenty fresh agents would carry out a questionable step. A system that can keep asking new agents will eventually find one that does.

Three claims I keep separate Software can preserve state across sessions. Suitable successor agents can continue from that state. A concealed, self-sustaining operation that secures its own money and compute and resists shutdown is a far stronger claim, and the first two do not establish it. The first two are enough to change how we think about control.

Notice what this continuity does not require. It does not require open weights. A saved instruction can steer an agent using a closed service, a locally run model, or a provider that does not exist yet. It can sit in storage after access to a particular model ends, although storage alone cannot wake it up. Which brings us, finally, to the debate everyone started with.

5

What openness actually changes

Now we can have the open-versus-closed argument in a more useful place.

The distinction that matters is between access to a service and access to the weights. A closed model can be used by hundreds of millions of people through a chatbot while its dials stay on the provider's computers. An open-weight model can be downloaded and run on your own hardware, if you have enough of it; a small model fits on a laptop, a frontier-scale one needs a room of specialized machines. Open-weight and open-source are also different things. Weights alone do not meet the Open Source Initiative's definition, which also calls for training code and meaningful information about the data.

Here is what openness does and does not change for the picture in the last section. The notebook does not care whose model reads it. A persistent operation can recruit a closed model through an API, a local open model, or a model nobody has built yet. Keeping weights closed does not prevent shared memory, inherited objectives, or repeated recruitment.

What closing the weights preserves is a breaker. A provider can cut off an account, watch for unusual patterns across many users, and update safeguards in one place. If an operation depends on one provider, pulling the breaker stops it, or at least puts it to sleep until someone connects it to another model. Open weights remove that breaker. Nobody can recall the copies, refusal behavior can be trained out, and the number of instances is limited only by hardware. The combination I would worry about most is a persistent mission plus an open frontier model plus broad tools, because the record supplies continuity and the weights supply intelligence nobody can switch off.

And yet the strongest case for open weights is also real, and it has little to do with "the crowd will police itself." Defenders analyzing an attack need a model they control, one that will not refuse to look at malware and will not ship their logs to a third party. Independent researchers cannot verify a lab's safety claims from the outside of an API. Permanent, opaque control of a foundational technology by a few companies is a risk in its own right, and nothing about a closed model guarantees the company behind it is transparent, accountable, or competent. Access to weights supports all of that.

So believing AI could cause catastrophe does not, by itself, tell you where to stand on openness. It tells you to ask about the capability, the use, and the controls around the deployment. Below some capability threshold, open weights look like the better bet for almost everyone. Above it, the irreversibility starts to dominate, and staged or licensed access looks more like how we handle dangerous biological materials than how we handle software. Either way, the model is one layer of the system, and the debate has been treating it as the whole thing.

6

Govern the operation, not just the model

A model becomes consequential through the permissions, memory, tools, and institutions around it. For any serious deployment I think we should be able to answer five questions, and the diagram shows where each one attaches.

  1. What can it do? Evaluate the whole deployed system, tools and repeated attempts included. A model tested on its own tells you about one path through the tree in Figure 8.
  2. Whose authority does it carry? Define legitimate goals and limits up front, and decide how a conflict between instructions gets escalated to a person rather than resolved quietly by the system.
  3. What can it change? Enforce permissions in software and in the services the system touches. An instruction in a prompt is a request; a boundary in the harness is a fact.
  4. What can it preserve or pass on? Know who wrote every shared record and with what authority. Treat messages from other agents as untrusted input, the way a good engineer treats anything arriving from the internet.
  5. Who can stop it? Control execution, budgets, credentials, and the resources that could restart it. Ending a session is different from ending an operation.

Controls should match stakes. Drafting a document and managing a hospital's network should not carry the same permissions because they happen to use the same model. Human review has to come with enough time, information, and independence to mean something, or it is a signature on a form.

None of this needs certainty about the future. It needs a credible pathway to harm, honesty about uncertainty, and action proportionate to both. The goal is to keep the enormous benefits of these systems while making dangerous operations harder to start, harder to scale, and harder to sustain.

How intelligent AI becomes matters less than what we let it do, and whether we can still step in.

A word about consciousness

I majored in philosophy and I have taught it, and I find the question of whether these systems experience anything genuinely fascinating. I also think it is irrelevant here. A system capable of contributing to a catastrophe warrants serious controls whether its expressions of concern reflect an inner life or nothing at all. Its capacities, its actions, and their consequences are enough to make this our problem.
